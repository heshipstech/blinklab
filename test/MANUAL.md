# Manual test script

A numbered checklist for anything that needs real eyes. Run the relevant items before merging a UI change, and the whole list before every phase tag.

1. (0.2) Run `npm run dev`, open the printed local URL. The page heading reads "Alertness demo" and the browser tab title reads "blinklab". No errors in the browser console.
2. (1.1, tightened by the fix for #22) Click "Start camera". The browser asks for camera permission. Allow it: your own face appears as live video within a second or two, with natural proportions matching what FaceTime shows. The button disappears once video runs.
3. (1.2) Reload the page and click "Start camera" again, this time deny the permission. A full sentence appears explaining that permission was denied and how to allow it again in browser settings. The page does not go blank, the button stays available, and the console shows no uncaught error.
4. (1.3, relabelled by remediation D1) The "Processing rate" line shows a steady number near your screen's refresh rate, usually 60 or 120. That is the instrument's own pace, not the camera's delivery rate, and the line says so. Drag-resize the window vigorously: the number dips, then recovers within about two seconds.
5. (1.4) Start the camera. The live picture looks identical to item 2, same proportions, updates live, no flicker. Proof it is now our drawing: right-click the picture, the menu offers image options ("Save Image As..."), not video playback controls.
6. (1.5) With more than one camera available (an iPhone via Continuity counts): after starting, a dropdown lists the cameras by name, and choosing another one switches the picture within a second or two. With a single camera, no dropdown appears at all.
7. (1.6) With the camera running, the Mirror box is ticked by default and the picture behaves like a bathroom mirror: lean to your left, your image leans to the same side of the screen. Untick it: the picture flips horizontally. The "Camera resolution" line states the real capture size, for example 1280 x 720, and matches what the picker's chosen camera delivers.
8. (2.1) Open the browser console (View, Developer, JavaScript Console), start the camera, sit in frame: within a few seconds the console prints "face detected: true". Cover the camera with a hand: "face detected: false". Uncover: "true" again. The lines appear only on change, not sixty times a second. The network tab shows no requests to any google domain. Expected and harmless: a handful of engine startup lines about xnnpack, WebGL and a "TensorFlow Lite XNNPACK delegate" INFO that Safari styles like an error. They are the model's boot chatter, not failures.
9. (2.2, superseded by 2.3) The full 478 dot mask is history: from 2.3 on, only the eye region carries dots.
10. (2.3) With your face in frame, exactly two rings of green dots trace your eyelid rims, about 16 dots per eye. Nothing on brows, lips or jaw. Blink slowly: each ring collapses and reopens with the lid. Wink: only that eye's ring collapses. Toggle Mirror: rings follow their eyes.
11. (2.4) Each iris additionally wears a small orange ring with a centre dot, clearly a different colour from the green eyelid dots. Look left, right, up, down without moving your head: the orange rings slide with your irises while the green dots stay put. The rings sit on the coloured part of the eye, not on the white.
12. (2.5) Not manually reproducible, by design: the wrong landmark count state cannot appear with the model file bundled by ADR-0002, which always returns 478 points. The guard defends against a future model swap and is covered by unit tests with a 468 point fixture. Nothing to check by hand, this entry documents why.
13. (2.6) With the camera running, an "Inference time" line shows a steady number in milliseconds beside the 30 ms budget. On a modern laptop it should sit well under 30. Record the number in docs/log.md. If it persistently exceeds the budget, open an issue with the number and the machine.
14. (2.7) Development server only: a "Record fixture" button appears below the readouts once the camera runs. Click it: it counts to 300 over about five seconds, then downloads session-01.json. Confirm the button does NOT exist on the public site, where the production build strips it.
15. (3.1) With eyes open, both "Eye aspect ratio" numbers sit steady, typically 0.25 to 0.45 depending on distance and expression. Close one eye: only that side drops to roughly half its open value or less while the other holds. The absolute values are personal, the separation is the test. Blink: both dip and recover. Cover the camera: the line disappears with the rest of the readouts. (Observed 2026-07-31: wink read 0.48 versus 0.24 at close range, the model under reports closure on winks, which is one reason 4.2 learns a personal baseline. Note 2026-08-11: readings before this date were inflated about 1.8x by a coordinate-space defect fixed after the audit, so that observation's absolute values are in the old scale; the halving relationship it records still holds, because at level head the same near-constant factor applies to both readings and cancels in their ratio.)
16. (3.2) A thin blue sparkline scrolls under the readouts, ten seconds wide, mostly flat while you sit still. Blink: a sharp valley appears and rides left across the chart. Cover the camera for two seconds and uncover: the line shows a real hole for that period, not a zero and not a flat bridge. The EAR text also reads "no valid measurement" while covered.
17. (3.4) The "Eyelid aperture" line reads a plausible millimetre value per eye, typically 7 to 12 mm with eyes open. The master check: lean in 20 cm toward the camera and back out. Your eyes grow and shrink hugely on screen, but the millimetre numbers should hold within roughly 1 mm. Pixels lie, the iris does not.
18. (3.5) The "Aperture stability" line shows two coefficients of variation side by side. Sit still for ten seconds: both CVs settle low, a few percent. Then lean in and out repeatedly for ten seconds without blinking much: the px CV should run several times the mm CV. The pass is the ratio, not an absolute. (Observed 2026-07-31: px 39 to 46% versus mm 13 to 17%, about three to one. The mm CV was not single digit because the owner's eyes genuinely widened when leaning in, EAR 0.53 to 0.60 in the pre-2026-08-11 inflated scale, roughly 0.30 to 0.34 after the coordinate fix, and very close range bends the flat face assumption behind the iris ruler.)
19. (3.6) The "Head pose" line shows three angles near zero when you face the camera squarely. The single axis test: nod slowly, only pitch should swing, tens of degrees. Shake your head, only yaw. Tilt ear to shoulder, only roll. Small bleed into the other two angles is normal, tens of degrees of bleed is a bug. Note the SIGNS you observe for nod down, shake left, tilt left, and report them, they calibrate 3.7 and 3.8. (Observed 2026-07-31: tilt toward the left shoulder reads positive roll, clean isolation at 42 degrees.)
20. (3.7) Tilt your head slowly toward a shoulder to roughly 30 degrees while keeping your eyes relaxed: the roll angle tracks the tilt while both millimetre apertures hold within about half a millimetre. There is no correction running, the measurement is built from distances, which rotation cannot change.
21. (3.8) Turn your head well past comfortable, beyond about 25 degrees of yaw: a sentence appears naming the axis, the angle and the limit, the EAR and aperture lines read "no valid measurement", and the sparkline records a gap. The head pose line keeps working so you can steer back. Face the camera again: numbers resume within a frame, the explanation sentence vanishes.
22. (4.1) Blink 10 times deliberately: the Blinks counter rises by exactly 10. Hold one blink closed for two seconds: it counts once, on the reopen. Two known blind spots, on record until their increments: a slow deep squint can fool it (4.7), and the 4 mm threshold is tuned to the owner's eyes, not everyone's (4.2).
23. (4.2) After starting the camera, a countdown runs: "Learning your open eyes: N s left", thirty seconds, sit normally and blink naturally. When it finishes, a line states your personal threshold, half of your measured baseline, plausibly close to half of your usual aperture reading. Then the never-falls check: squint moderately for a sustained ten seconds and watch the baseline line, the numbers must NOT drop. Restarting the camera restarts the learning.
24. (4.3, amended by 4.7) Blink normally: the Blinks line gains "(last: N ms)", typically 80 to 200 ms for a natural blink. A slightly slow deliberate blink, under half a second, reads longer than a quick one. A closure held beyond half a second no longer counts as a blink at all, see item 28.
25. (4.4) The Blinks line shows "rate: measuring..." for the first 15 seconds, then a per minute number. Sit at rest doing nothing for a minute: it should settle somewhere near 10 to 25, most people read 15 to 20. Then hold a staring contest with the camera for 20 seconds: the rate visibly falls. Concentration suppresses blinking, which is itself a Phase 5 attention signal in embryo.
26. (4.5) After each blink, the "Last blink shape" line updates: amplitude a few millimetres, peak closing velocity tens of mm per second, A/V somewhere around 30 to 150 ms. The shape test: blink once naturally, note the numbers, then close your eyes SLOWLY and deliberately. The slow blink must show a clearly lower peak velocity and a higher A/V. Same amplitude, different speed: that difference is what drowsiness looks like in the literature.
27. (4.6) Hard to trigger on a healthy machine, by design: if the frame rate ever drops below 25 fps, the Blinks line is replaced by a sentence naming the current fps and the 25 fps minimum, and no blink numbers are shown, null, never zero. The gate is fully unit tested; if you ever see this sentence in real use, it is working, not broken. A heavily loaded machine or low power mode may reproduce it.
28. (4.7) The master script's own squint check: hold a squint, eyes nearly or fully shut, for a full five seconds, then relax. The Blinks counter must NOT move, and the last duration must not change. Only brief closures, half a second or less below threshold, count as blinks. Long holds are a different phenomenon, detected separately at 6.2.
29. (4.8) Blink several times over a minute. Below the sparkline, a list grows: one line per blink, newest on top, each showing seconds since the session started, the duration, and the shape numbers (or "shape unavailable"). After many blinks the list scrolls inside its box rather than growing the page. Restarting the camera clears it.
30. (5.1) The "Iris offset" line shows two signed pairs, horizontal / vertical per eye, hovering near +0.00 while you look at the camera. Hold your head still and look far left, far right, up, down: the horizontal numbers should swing roughly plus and minus 0.05 to 0.15 together on both eyes, the vertical likewise for up and down. Note which direction of YOUR gaze reads positive and record it here for 5.2. Looking at the screen centre should read near zero again.
31. (5.2) Head still, look deliberately at each corner of your screen: the "Looking toward" line should name the correct quadrant most of the time. Two honest caveats, on record: everything is uncalibrated until 5.4, and a camera sitting above the screen biases the vertical split toward "bottom", so the top quadrants may need a slightly exaggerated upward glance. If left and right ever read systematically SWAPPED, that is a sign bug, report it.
32. (5.3) Keep your head facing the camera and glance well past the screen's edge, over your shoulder line or at the desk beside you: the "Looking toward" line switches to "off screen". Look back at the screen: a quadrant returns within a frame. Ordinary corner glances must NOT trigger it, only genuine look aways.
33. (5.4a) Click "Calibrate gaze". A dark overlay appears with one orange dot. Follow it with your eyes, head still: it visits nine positions, pausing at each, roughly 30 to 60 seconds total. If you look away or blink a lot, a position simply takes longer, invalid frames never count. When it finishes, the overlay closes and the button reads "Recalibrate gaze (samples stored)". Clicking the overlay cancels without storing. The samples stay in this browser only, they never leave the device.
34. (5.4b) Run a calibration (item 33) sitting as you normally sit. When the overlay closes, the button reads "Recalibrate gaze" and the "Looking toward" line now ends in "(calibrated)". The amended 5.4 target, checked with your own eyes: head reasonably still, look deliberately at each corner of the screen and hold for a second, the line should name the correct quadrant reliably, INCLUDING the top ones that previously needed an exaggerated upward glance, since the profile absorbs the camera-above bias. Reload the page and start the camera again: the profile survives (it lives in this browser's local storage), the button already reads "Recalibrate gaze" and quadrants stay calibrated from the first frame. Note: "off screen" keeps its "(uncalibrated)" tag on purpose, that boundary is still the guessed 5.3 threshold, its calibration is a recorded follow-up issue.
35. (5.6) Below the EAR sparkline, two new charts run while the camera does: horizontal and vertical gaze offset over 10 seconds, thin gray for raw, bold orange for smoothed. Stare at one spot with your head still: the gray line is a fuzzy band, the orange line through it is nearly flat. Now glance quickly left and right: both lines jump together, the orange lands within a frame or two of the gray, no visible lag. That pair of observations IS the jitter-lag tradeoff, escaped. Reading directions: glance toward YOUR right and the horizontal trace rises, look up and the vertical trace rises. Blink: the vertical trace notches briefly downward in BOTH lines. That is deliberate, the filter follows fast real events, and separating blinks and saccades from steady gaze is 5.7's business. Turn your head past the pose gate or look far off screen: both lines break into a gap and restart fresh, gaps stay gaps.
36. (5.7) A new "Gaze state" line runs under the quadrant line. Stare at one spot, head still: it reads "fixating for N s" and N climbs steadily. Sweep your eyes to a different spot: it flickers to "moving" for a beat, then fixates again from zero. The beat has a reason: stillness only becomes a fixation after 120 milliseconds inside the box, so every jump costs one visible moment of "moving". Now read a paragraph of text naturally: the line alternates quickly between the two states, that alternation is your scanpath, the thing 5.9's heatmap will draw. Blink or turn your head past the gate: the state resets, a fixation never bridges a gap.
37. (5.8) A "Fixations in the last 10 s" line runs under the gaze state: count, mean, median and longest duration. Two experiments, opposite signatures. First read a paragraph of text naturally: the count climbs toward maybe 15 to 30 and the mean and median settle somewhere near 150 to 300 ms, typical reading fixations. Then stare hard at one single point: the count drops to a few, and the longest climbs live while you hold, the still-growing fixation counts at its length so far on purpose. If mean and median ever disagree strongly, one unusually long fixation is sitting in the window, which is the disagreement working as designed. This item verifies the ninth ritual's prediction: compare your predicted reading fixation duration to the median the panel shows.
38. (5.9) Calibrate first (item 33), then click "Gaze heatmap" (it stays disabled until a profile exists). A dark full screen card appears with five outlined shapes. Look at each shape and hold for two or three seconds: an orange glow builds in the cells where you dwell, brightest where you held longest. The glow should land on or beside the shape you actually watched, one or two cells of scatter is the calibration's honest error, not a bug (the grid is quadrant-coarse on purpose). Glance quickly across the card: passing cells barely warm. Blink or turn your head: nothing accumulates during untrusted frames. Click anywhere to close, reopening starts a fresh card.
39. (5.10) Run a heatmap session (item 38), looking at three or four shapes in an order you can remember, then close it. The "Replay scanpath" button is now enabled (it stays disabled until a session has recorded a path). Click it: the card returns with a slider at the bottom, fully scrubbed to the end, showing your whole path as a thin line, orange circles around the spots where you parked (bigger circle, longer hold), and a dot at the final position. Drag the slider slowly left: the path unwinds in reverse, circles disappear newest first, and the top line counts the seconds. Scrub back and forth: the order you remember looking in is the order the line travels. Click anywhere outside the slider to close. Starting a new heatmap session or restarting the camera clears the recording.
40. (6.1) After the 30 second baseline learns, a PERCLOS line appears reading "measuring..." for about 15 more seconds, then a percentage. Sit normally for a minute: it should settle in the low single digits, often between one and three percent, your blinks added up. Now close your eyes fully for a slow count of six and reopen: within a few seconds the number visibly jumps (six closed seconds in a sixty second window adds about ten points), then drains back down over the next minute as the closure slides out of the window. The drain is the rolling window made visible. This item verifies the tenth ritual's prediction: compare your predicted eyes-closed percentage to the resting number. (Amended by fix #113: the closed line is now the measured 40 percent shut line, so the resting number reads somewhat higher than under the original unreachable 20 percent line, and the six second closure test actually registers, which it provably did not before.)
41. (6.2, amended by amendment 5) A "Long closures" line sits under PERCLOS, reading "waiting for the baseline" until the 30 second learn finishes: the detector has no personal shut line before that, so wait for it. Blink normally for a while: the count must stay at 0. A relaxed droop, lids low while reading, also stays at 0 since amendment 5. Close your eyes FULLY and hold: after half a second, the moment the closure outgrows a blink, the line reads "eyes closed N s and counting", live, while your eyes are still shut, and the count has already risen by one. Reopen: the line switches to "last: N ms" with the full held duration. Hold a five second closure: the count rises by exactly one, not once per second. This is the same experiment as item 28, seen from the other side: there the Blinks counter refused the hold, here the long closure counter claims it. Since amendment 5 the bins are three and named: brief blink, partial droop (the band between the two lines, deliberately no event), and eyes shut. A closure that reaches truly shut lands in exactly one of blink or long closure by its duration.
42. (6.3) Hold a long closure (item 41): the moment it fires, an orange banner appears reading "Alert: long eye closure" with fired and suppressed counts, and disappears about three seconds later. Now fire two long closures within five seconds: the first raises the banner, the second is swallowed, the suppressed count rises by one when the banner next shows. Wait six quiet seconds and hold again: the banner returns, the alert count rises. The restraint is the feature: a demo measurement that nags would teach you to ignore it. Restarting the camera clears both counts.
43. (amendment 5) The owner's regression check, both directions, run only after the 30 second baseline learns. First: read something at the very bottom of the screen for a slow ten seconds, lids naturally low. The Long closures count must NOT rise and no alert may appear (before the amendment this produced multi-second false closures and repeated alerts). Second: close your eyes fully for a slow count of three and reopen. The count rises by exactly one and the last duration lands near three seconds. Note: closing "softly", lids touching but not pressed, may read as only a partial droop on some faces, the line is deliberately deep. Since fix #113 PERCLOS shares the shut line, so during the second direction of this check it must visibly rise within a few seconds of the closure and drain as the window slides, no longer 0.0%.
44. (amendment 5, known limitation on record) A slow drowsy descent spends time in the droop band before reaching truly shut, and that band time does not yet count toward the closure clock, so an alert trails the true onset by the descent time, and a closure whose truly-shut span stays under half a second is missed entirely. This is the depth-qualified episode redesign, filed as an issue, not a surprise. The shut line also freezes at the first learned baseline of each camera session, and since fix #113 BOTH shut-line consumers, long closures and PERCLOS, derive from that one frozen number, so they can never disagree about the line mid session. A session that learned while unusually wide or droopy eyed keeps that line until restart: if long closures or PERCLOS seem dead or twitchy all session, restart the camera and let the baseline relearn while sitting normally.
45. (fix #114) The chatter check. After the baseline learns, read for half a minute at the bottom of the screen, the posture that used to flood the blink log. The log must stay clean: no sub-50 ms entries with amplitudes near 0.1 mm, and the Blinks count must not creep without real blinks. Then blink deliberately five times: the count rises by exactly five, a real blink plunges far past the arm line and always counts. Durations are unchanged from every old recording. One documented cost: a deliberately SHALLOW blink, lids barely dipping below the line, may not count, it is indistinguishable from deep chatter, on record in LEARNING.
46. (fix #113) The blindness regression, the exact experiment that found the bug, now run as verification. Wait for PERCLOS to show a percentage, then close your eyes fully for a slow count of six and reopen. Before the fix this provably left PERCLOS at 0.0%. Now it must jump to ten percent or a little above within a few seconds, then drain back toward its resting value over the next minute as the closure slides out of the window. The resting value itself sits higher than the old zero, low single digits, your blinks finally being counted as the closed time they are.
47. (6.4) A "Feature records" line appears under the alert area, counting up by roughly one per second while the camera runs. Cover the camera with your hand for five seconds: the count keeps rising, those seconds produce honest null rows with faceDetected false, absence is data. Restart the camera: the count restarts from zero. After an hour of continuous running the wording changes to "last 3600 kept, oldest discarded", the buffer admitting its cap instead of claiming the full session. Nothing else is visible yet: the rows exist for the 6.5 score and the 6.7 export, and this line is only the heartbeat proving they accumulate.
48. (6.5) A bold "Alertness score" line sits near the top, reading "measuring..." until PERCLOS exists (about 45 seconds), then a number out of 100 with the demo disclaimer beside it, updating about once per second. Sit normally and blink normally: exactly 100, or within a point or two of it. This is the load bearing check of the whole increment: every penalty ramp starts ABOVE the ranges items 24, 26 and 40 record as normal for your eyes, so ordinary blinking must cost nothing. If sitting still and awake ever costs you points, the ramps are mispriced and that is a bug, not fatigue. Cover the camera: the line reads "no face in frame" rather than scoring an empty chair. Close your eyes for a slow six count: within a few seconds the score visibly drops, your closure charging both the eyes closed share and a long closure, then climbs back as the minute forgets. The score can never leave 0 to 100: the four penalty caps sum to exactly one hundred, and 6.6's panel will soon show which signals drove any drop. The disclaimer is permanent and deliberate: demo, not a safety or medical device.
49. (6.6) Under the alertness score, a short panel explains it. Sitting normally and awake it reads "Nothing is costing points." with an empty list, which is the correct and boring answer. Before your first blink of the session it adds "2 signals unavailable", because both blink shape signals need a blink to exist: absence is stated, never quietly counted as alertness. Now close your eyes for a slow six count: the score drops and the panel names why, biggest first, so "eyes closed share" leads with roughly twenty points and "long closures" follows with fifteen. Watch it recover over the next minute as the window forgets. One deliberate limit: the panel shows at most three drivers even though the score has four parts, because three lines get read and four get skipped, and the hidden one is never larger than the smallest one shown (on a tie it can be exactly equal).
50. (6.7) An "Export CSV" button sits under the feature records line, greyed out until the first record exists. Run a session for a minute or two, then click it: a file named blinklab-session-<number>.csv downloads to this device. Open it in a spreadsheet. The first row is sixteen column names starting with timestampMs, and below it one row per second. Check three things by eye: the columns line up (no row is shifted, which is what a comma bug looks like), seconds where you covered the camera show faceDetected false with most other cells EMPTY rather than zero, and the numbers match what the page was showing at that time. The file never leaves your device, there is no server to send it to.
51. (6.8) Start the camera: before anything else a short panel asks "Before you begin: how sleepy do you feel?" with nine numbered options and a Skip button. Pick one, or skip, and it disappears. Run a session, then click Export CSV: the panel returns asking "How sleepy do you feel now?", and the download happens once you answer or skip. Open the file: the first two lines are "# kss_before:" and "# kss_after:" with your answers and their wording, above the column header. Check the skip path too: skipping must write "skipped", never a number, because a refusal is not a five. The anchors are the published Karolinska wording and must not be reworded; if they ever read differently from a sleep study you find, that is a bug worth reporting.
52. (6.9) Load the page without starting the camera: an orange bordered notice sits above everything, saying this is a demo and not a safety or medical device, that it is not diagnostic, that your video and measurements never leave your browser, and that the bundled face model does send usage statistics to Google. Try to get rid of it: click it, click around it, start and stop the camera. It must never disappear. Then check the README on GitHub shows the same sentence near the top, and the alertness score line still carries its own short parenthetical, because a number and its caveat should travel together when someone screenshots it.
53. (layout) Load the page on your own monitor. The demo notice is a full width grey bar directly under the graph strip, centred and in normal weight. It is quieter than it was: if it ever stops reading as a disclaimer to a stranger, that is worth reconsidering, since increment 6.9's brief was that it be prominent as well as permanent. The mirror toggle sits on the same line as the camera resolution. The eyelid tracking dots draw white rather than green, and are now OFF by default behind an "Eye markers" checkbox: tick it and check they stay visible against your glasses reflections, and say so if they wash out, because the whole point of that overlay is confirming the model has found your eyes. The readouts sit in a centred column about 1280 pixels wide rather than hugging the far left, so reading them barely turns your head. Start the camera: the sparkline and the two gaze traces appear as a full width strip across the very top, sized to your window, and they resize if you resize the window. This is the arrangement for screenshots: capture the top of the page and you get the traces plus as many readouts as fit. Before the camera starts, the graph strip must take no space at all, no empty black band.
54. (fix #122) Sit for a minute so PERCLOS shows a number, then close your eyes for several seconds and immediately cover the camera or leave the frame while they are still closed. Watch the PERCLOS line: it must go back to "measuring..." within about two seconds rather than continuing to show a number, and it certainly must not show a number that keeps rising while you are not there. Uncover the camera and it resumes within a frame. The alertness score already refused during absence, this is the underlying measurement finally refusing too.
55. (fix #126) The wide eyed test. Start a session, sit normally through the learning window, then deliberately raise your eyebrows and open your eyes as wide as you can for two or three seconds, then relax. Watch the "Personal blink threshold" line: it may rise a little, but it must stay comfortably below your ordinary open aperture, which the "Eyelid aperture" line shows. Then blink normally ten times and check the log: durations should read near your usual 120 to 200 ms, not 300 or more, and amplitudes should stay in millimetres rather than collapsing toward zero. Before this fix a couple of seconds of surprise could lift the threshold above your resting eye for the rest of the session, and every blink measured afterwards was wrong.
56. (7.0) The clip test, which needs a recording of a face. Load the page and, without starting the camera at all, use "Or measure a recorded clip" to pick a video of someone's face. First watch the order of events: the status line says it is loading the model BEFORE the clip starts, and the clip stays still until that finishes. That wait is deliberate. Playing during it would silently eat the first few seconds of the recording with no way to get them back, which review measured at about three and a half seconds. Then the clip plays, the line that normally reports camera resolution instead reads "Clip: <your filename>, <width> x <height> pixels" and a length, the eyelid dots track the face in it, and the readouts fill in as they do live. No sleepiness question appears at any point, neither at the start nor when you export, and that is deliberate: your answer would be written into the file as the recorded person's label. When the clip ends the status says so and invites you to export or pick another. Now the check that matters. Note the clip's real length, then export the CSV and open it. The last timestampMs must be close to the clip's length in milliseconds, NOT the time you spent watching. The first two lines must read "# source: file" and "# clip: <your filename>". Then start the camera normally and export again: those lines must read "# source: camera" and "# clip: none", with no trace of the previous clip's name, and the sleepiness questions must come back. Three more things review found and fixed, each worth confirming once. Pick the SAME clip a second time: it must run again rather than doing nothing. Watch the "Last blink shape" line right after a clip starts: it must be blank until the clip's own first blink, never still showing a shape measured from your camera session. And check the reported frames per second against what you know the clip to be: if your machine cannot run the model fast enough it will read lower, and that is honest rather than wrong, but it is recorded as issue #145 because it means a slow machine measures less of a clip than a fast one.
57. (7.4) The stepping test, using the same clip as check 56. Leave "Measure every frame" ticked, which is how it arrives, and pick your clip. It does not play: it seeks from frame to frame and looks like a slideshow, which is correct rather than broken. The status reports progress and, at the end, how many frames it measured AND the frame rate it detected. That rate is the check: compare it against what you know your clip to be. If you recorded at 60 and it says 60, every frame was seen. If it says 30, half of them were missed and something is wrong. This exact check is why the increment had to be rebuilt: the first version played the clip and paused on each frame, which cannot outrun a video advancing in real time, and it measured 6,655 frames of a 12,626 frame recording while reporting "every frame". Now untick the box and run the same clip again. It plays normally and looks much better, and the reported count will be lower, on a busy machine much lower. Export both CSVs and compare the header block: one says "# measurement_mode: stepped" with a "# measured_fps" matching your clip, the other says "played" with a lower one. Use ticked for anything you intend to trust, and unticked for anything you intend to film.
58. (Safari) Run any clip in Safari as well as Chrome. Safari behaves differently from Chrome in two ways that both bit this project, so it is worth one deliberate pass. The first: Safari does not fetch a video's data until something asks for it, and a stepped run never plays, so every seek waited for bytes that were never requested and the whole run measured ZERO frames while reporting it as a result. The second: Safari signals a finished seek differently, so the code now waits for either signal rather than one. If a clip ever reports zero frames, or the count is far below what your clip contains, that is this class of bug returning and it is worth reporting immediately. The automated suite now runs the clip tests in WebKit as well as Chromium, which is what should catch it first, but Safari proper is not WebKit proper and only you can check the real thing.
59. (blink log) Run a clip with the step box ticked, then click "Export blink log", which sits beside "Export CSV" and stays greyed out until a blink exists. Open the file. One row per blink, and the first two columns are the frame numbers the closure spanned. Check them against the duration column: at 60 frames per second a 133 ms blink should span about 8 frames, and a 50 ms one about 3. If the frame span and the duration disagree, the clock and the frame counter have come apart and that is worth reporting. Then run the live camera and export again: the frame columns must be EMPTY, because a frame number from a webcam session means nothing to anyone. Finally check a blink whose shape could not be measured, which happens near the start of a session: amplitude and velocity must be empty rather than zero, since a blink of zero amplitude is a real and very different claim about your eyelid.
60. (layout) Load the page with nothing running. You should see the demo notice, the title, and ONE box called Source. No graph strip, no empty boxes, no frame counter. Two things used to leak through here and both are worth re-checking: a frames per second line reporting your MONITOR's refresh rate as though it were the instrument's, and the blink log export button. If either appears before you start a source, the visibility rules have drifted again. Then start a camera or a clip. Boxes should appear in this order: Source, the video, Alertness, then Blinks, Eyes and Gaze side by side, then Session and Instrument in smaller type at the bottom. On a narrow window the three middle boxes should stack rather than squash. The alertness score is the only large text on the page, with the short disclaimer directly beneath it in small grey type: check that a screenshot of the score cannot be taken without the disclaimer coming with it, because that was 6.9's whole point. Finally, the page background must be white with dark text whatever your system theme is. Before this the page set no colours at all, so a Mac in dark mode rendered black text on a black background, which is why earlier screenshots were so hard to read.
61. (overlays) Start a camera or a clip. By default the face should be clean: no dots, no rings, nothing drawn on it. Two checkboxes sit beside Mirror. Tick "Eye markers" and the white eyelid dots and orange iris rings appear, which is how you confirm the model has actually found your eyes rather than guessing. Tick "Face mesh" and all 478 landmarks appear as faint grey dots across the whole face. Untick both and the picture must be clean again immediately, within a frame. Worth knowing what you gave up: with the markers off you cannot see at a glance whether the model is tracking, so if a number looks wrong the first thing to do is tick "Eye markers" and look. This trade was made deliberately, because a face with dots on it is not what anyone wants to watch in a demo.
62. (blink calibration) Start the camera and, in the Blinks box, click "Calibrate blinks". A dark overlay fills the screen reading "Keep your eyes OPEN and look at the screen" with a countdown and the note that closing comes next. Hold your eyes open. After three seconds it switches to "Now gently CLOSE your eyes and hold them shut"; close them and hold until the overlay closes (another three seconds). A status line under the button then reports your blink line in millimetres, the midpoint of your measured open and closed aperture, and says it was saved for your next visit and does NOT change detection yet. Reload the page and start the camera: the button now reads "Recalibrate blinks", because the line survived in this browser's local storage. Two refusals to check on purpose: click "Calibrate blinks" and immediately click the overlay to cancel, nothing is stored and the button still reads "Calibrate blinks"; and run a calibration without ever closing your eyes (hold them open through both phases), the status refuses with "did not read far enough below your open ones" rather than inventing a line. The line stays in this browser only, it never leaves the device, and the "Stored on this device" box lists it and the erase button removes it.
