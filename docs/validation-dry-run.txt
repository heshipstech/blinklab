The validation round's DRY RUN, 16 to 17 August 2026
====================================================

Five sessions, one face, the owner's, on three devices. This is NOT the
six-person round. It is the rehearsal that had to happen before six
strangers were asked, and its purpose was to find what fixtures cannot.
It did, and it also refuted one of its own first conclusions.

The raw CSVs are NOT in this repository. They sit in
$DATASETS/validation-dry-run. Reproduce this table with:

What this found
===============

1. THE MEASUREMENT TRAVELS BETWEEN DEVICES. WHAT IS LEARNED FROM IT
   DOES NOT.

   The same face gave a median eyelid aperture within 11 percent across
   all five sessions. The iris normalisation works: it is doing exactly
   the job it was built for, cancelling distance and resolution.

   The baselines learned from those measurements were 7.69, 8.09, 9.80,
   7.78 and 7.30 mm, a spread of 34 percent.

   P3 is the outlier and P4 is the same laptop done again. In P3 a
   handful of frames in the first 30 seconds read up to 10.35 mm against
   a window median of 7.51. The baseline is a 90th percentile, so it
   followed them and settled at 1.41 times that session's own median
   aperture, which put its blink line at 71 percent of resting instead
   of 56. It then counted 26.0 blinks per minute, and one of its
   detections has an amplitude of 1.26 mm, below the 1.5 mm line the
   on-screen table greys out as too faint to be a blink.

   The baseline check as pre-registered could not see any of that.
   Readiness passed on all five. Drift passed on all five, INCLUDING
   P3, because a baseline born wrong does not move: its drift is 0.0.
   The plan's second dated correction adds `baseline_over_resting`,
   flagged above 1.25, and P3 is the only session it flags.

2. AT ABOUT 30 FRAMES PER SECOND THIS INSTRUMENT MISSES DELIBERATE
   BLINKS, AND THE 25 FPS GATE NEVER FIRES.

   Take only the four sessions whose baseline is sound, so the
   threshold is not doing the work:

       P2  iPhone,  30.7 fps    7 of 10
       P4  MacBook, 29.2 fps    9 of 10
       P5  Sony,   126.7 fps   10 of 10

   The misses are not scattered. Each one shows up as a hole in an
   otherwise metronome cadence, and each hole is a whole number of
   blinks wide:

       P2, cadence 0.90 to 1.00 s, holes of 2.89 s and 2.98 s
       P4, cadence 1.32 to 1.60 s, one hole of 3.00 s

   The person did not pause. The instrument stopped reporting.

   Every one of those sessions ran above the 25 frames per second floor
   throughout the marked window, so nothing was refused and nothing
   said anything was wrong.

3. THE PROCESSING RATE IS SET BY THE COMPUTER, NOT BY THE CAMERA.

   All five sessions used a camera declaring 30 frames per second. The
   rate the page actually ran at was:

       4 cores   iPhone      31.7 and 30.7 fps
       4 cores   MacBook     32.4 and 29.2 fps
       12 cores  Sony        126.7 fps

   The one machine with twelve cores ran the model four times faster
   than the two with four, on cameras that all declare the same rate.
   The model's own speed is the ceiling, not the camera's delivery.

   Taken with finding 2 this is the uncomfortable part. How many blinks
   this instrument reports depends on how fast the viewer's computer
   is, and the page tells them nothing about it.

4. RETRACTED: "BLINK DURATION MOVES WITH THE PROCESSING RATE."

   The first version of this document, written on 16 August with four
   sessions, said blink duration tracks the rate, reading P2 at 96 ms
   and 30.7 fps against P5 at 129 ms and 126.7 fps.

   P4 refutes it. It is the SLOWEST session of the sound-baseline three
   and it reports the LONGEST blinks:

       P4  29.2 fps    149 ms
       P2  30.7 fps     96 ms
       P5 126.7 fps    129 ms

   That is not ordered by rate in either direction. The claim was built
   on two points that happened to line up, and the third point killed
   it. What is left is a smaller and still unexplained fact: the phone
   reports blinks about a third shorter than either Mac, while having
   twice the iris resolution of the MacBook.

5. WHAT THE DECISIVE TEST DID AND DID NOT SETTLE.

   P4 was run specifically to separate the processing rate from the
   device, by repeating P3's laptop at the same rate with a baseline
   that lands correctly. It did not settle it.

   P2 and P4 run at 30.7 and 29.2 frames per second, which is the same
   rate for this purpose, and they miss 3 and 1 of ten. A rate that is
   the same in both cannot explain a difference of three times. So
   something about the phone is worse than the laptop beyond the rate,
   and this dry run cannot say what: device, browser engine (the iPhone
   is WebKit, both Macs are Blink), camera, portrait against landscape
   and propped against fixed all still move together.

   The finding that survives needs neither question settled. At the
   rate a four core machine produces, whether laptop or phone, this
   instrument loses deliberate blinks, and the 25 fps floor is above
   none of it.

6. ONE ANOMALY THAT DID NOT REPEAT.

   P1 counted a single continuous six second closure as three long
   closures. P2 through P5 each counted theirs as one. Recorded because
   it happened, not diagnosed, and one occurrence in five is not enough
   to call it a defect.

7. THE TOOL ITSELF NEEDED NO FIXES.

   Ten real files across five sessions, no refusals, and the pairing
   coped with device names inserted into the filenames. That was not a
   foregone conclusion, and it is the one thing this rehearsal was
   expected to break.
