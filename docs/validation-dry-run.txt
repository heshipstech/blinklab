The validation round's DRY RUN, 16 to 19 August 2026
====================================================

Six sessions, one face, the owner's, on four devices. This is NOT the
six-person round. It is the rehearsal that had to happen before six
strangers were asked, and its purpose was to find what fixtures cannot.
It did, it refuted one of its own conclusions, and on 19 August it
settled the question it had earlier recorded as unsettled.

The raw CSVs are NOT in this repository. They sit in
$DATASETS/validation-dry-run. Reproduce this table with:

    cd analysis
    PYTHONPATH="$PWD" .venv/bin/python tools/validation_report.py \
        "$DATASETS/validation-dry-run"

Rows carry the session name as well as the label. Labels are POSITIONAL
and shift when a session is added: the MacBook re-test was P4 in the
17 August version of this file and is P5 here, which silently falsified
the prose around it for two days. Refer to sessions by name.

    iphone          iPhone 14 Pro Max, first run, marks in the wrong place
    iphone2         iPhone 14 Pro Max, re-run to protocol
    iphone17promax  iPhone 17 Pro Max
    macbookair      MacBook Air, first run, baseline learned too long
    macbookair2     MacBook Air, re-run with a baseline that landed
    pcsony          Sony A7 IV through a Cam Link 4K

Two sessions were repeated rather than deleted. `iphone`'s ten
deliberate blinks are not between its marks and the owner did not

`macbookair` learned a baseline 41 percent longer than its own session's

1. The detector does not generalise, at 3 or more missed among the 5 sound sessions: 3 missed (P1, P3, P5). FAILED Excluded as unsound: P4 (baseline is too long to be open).

BASELINE TOO LONG TO BE "OPEN", above 1.25 times the session's own median aperture: P4. The blink line is half the baseline, so in these sessions it sits above 62 percent of resting and partial closures are being counted as blinks. Readiness and drift do not see this, because a baseline born wrong does not move.


What this found
===============

1. THE MEASUREMENT TRAVELS BETWEEN DEVICES. WHAT IS LEARNED FROM IT
   DOES NOT.

   The same face gave a median eyelid aperture within about 11 percent
   across every session. The iris normalisation works: it cancels
   distance and resolution exactly as designed.

   The baselines learned from those measurements did not. `macbookair`
   settled at 1.41 times its own session's median aperture, which put
   its blink line at 71 percent of resting instead of the 56 to 58 the
   others managed, so a blink had only to close 29 percent of the way to
   register. That session logged 26.0 blinks per minute and one
   detection of 1.26 mm amplitude, below the 1.5 mm line the on-screen
   table greys out as too faint to be a blink.

   The baseline check as first pre-registered could not see any of it.
   Readiness passed on all six. Drift passed on all six INCLUDING that
   one, because a baseline born wrong does not move: its drift is 0.0.
   The plan's second dated correction adds `baseline_over_resting`,
   flagged above 1.25, and `macbookair` is the only session it flags.

2. AT ABOUT 30 FRAMES PER SECOND THIS INSTRUMENT LOSES DELIBERATE
   BLINKS. AT 55 AND ABOVE IT DOES NOT. THE 25 FPS GATE NEVER FIRES
   ANYWHERE IN BETWEEN.

   Only the sessions whose baseline is sound, so the threshold is not
   doing the work, and only the ones whose marks are where the protocol
   asks:

       iphone2         30.7 fps     7 of 10
       macbookair2     29.2 fps     9 of 10
       iphone17promax  55.0 fps    10 of 10
       pcsony         126.7 fps    10 of 10

   The misses are not scattered. Each shows up as a hole in an otherwise
   metronome cadence, a whole number of blinks wide: `iphone2` has two
   holes of 2.89 s and 2.98 s in a 1.0 s rhythm, `macbookair2` a single
   3.00 s hole in a 1.5 s rhythm. The person did not pause. The
   instrument stopped reporting.

   Every one of those sessions ran above the 25 frames per second floor
   throughout its marked window, so nothing was refused and nothing on
   the page said anything was wrong.

3. THE PROCESSING RATE IS SET BY THE COMPUTER, NOT BY THE CAMERA.

   Every camera in every session declares 30 frames per second. The rate
   the page actually achieved ranged from 29.2 to 126.7. It is the speed
   of the machine running the face model, not the delivery rate of the
   camera, and the page reports it as a readout rather than as a caveat
   on the numbers beside it.

   A caution against the tidy version of this. An earlier draft said
   twelve cores gives 127 fps. Participant 2 of the actual round has
   twelve cores and runs at 45. Core count alone does not predict it;
   frame size and machine both matter.

4. THE PREDICTION THAT SETTLED IT, COMMITTED BEFORE THE SESSION WAS RUN.

   The 17 August version of this document ended with a section titled
   "what the decisive test did not settle". Two sessions ran at 30.7 and
   29.2 frames per second and missed 3 and 1 of ten. A rate that is the
   same in both cannot explain a difference of three times, so something
   about the phone appeared to be worse beyond the rate, and device,
   browser engine, camera, orientation and mounting all moved together.

   Before `iphone17promax` was recorded, this was written down:

     If the processing rate is the cause, the iPhone 17 will run
     meaningfully faster than 30 fps and should catch close to 10 of 10.

     If it is something about iOS, WebKit or the front camera, it will
     miss roughly 3 of ten REGARDLESS of how fast it runs.

   It ran at 55.0 frames per second and caught 10 of 10, at a clean
   1.0 second cadence. The second hypothesis is refuted by the same
   session, which holds iOS, WebKit, the front-facing camera, portrait
   orientation and propped mounting constant against `iphone2`.

   The phone was never the problem. The rate was.

   This also agrees with the offline experiment in
   `docs/blink-sample-rate.txt`, which was run before either phone
   session and predicted the vulnerable band closes by 60 Hz. Field data
   and synthetic experiment now point the same way from independent
   directions.

   WHAT IS STILL CONFOUNDED, stated rather than buried. The camera is
   not identical: `Front Camera` on the 14, `Front Ultra Wide Camera` on
   the 17, though the iris scale is close at 44.6 against 42.7 pixels.
   iOS moved 26.5.2 to 26.6.1 and Chrome 151 to 152. It is one session
   per device on one face. The rate is the main thing that changed, not
   the only one. What makes the result worth something is that the
   prediction was committed first and the alternative was named.

5. STILL RETRACTED, AND MORE FIRMLY: "BLINK DURATION MOVES WITH THE
   PROCESSING RATE."

   The 16 August version of this document claimed it. The 17 August
   version retracted it. `iphone17promax` buries it. Median detected
   blink duration against processing rate, sound sessions only:

       macbookair2     29.2 fps    149 ms
       iphone2         30.7 fps     96 ms
       iphone17promax  55.0 fps     97 ms
       pcsony         126.7 fps    129 ms

   The iPhone nearly doubled its frame rate and reported the same blink
   duration to within a millisecond. Duration tracks the DEVICE, not the
   rate: both iPhones read about 96 ms, both Macs 149 to 166, the Sony
   129. Why the phone reads a third shorter than either Mac is
   unexplained and is not a sampling effect.

6. PHONES OVER-COUNT LONG CLOSURES, ON THREE OF THREE PHONE SESSIONS.

   `iphone` recorded 3 long closures for one continuous six second
   closure and `iphone17promax` recorded 2. Every per-second sample in
   the iPhone 17's closure sat between 2.79 and 3.01 mm against a shut
   line of 3.04, so the aperture hovered just under the line and noise
   re-crossed it repeatedly. Participant 3 of the round, an Android
   phone, did the same thing twice. All three desktop sessions here
   recorded exactly 1.

   That is the same shape as the ratcheted case in issue #178: a
   threshold sitting inside the signal's own wobble. Recorded, not
   diagnosed, and three phone sessions is a pattern rather than a proof.

7. THE TOOL NEEDED NO FIXES.

   Twelve real files across six sessions, no refusals, and the pairing
   coped with device names inserted into the filenames.
