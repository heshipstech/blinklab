Does the rate the page runs at change how many blinks it finds?
===============================================================

17 August 2026. A deterministic experiment, no camera and no people.

WHY. The dry run of 16 and 17 August gave 7, 9 and 10 of ten deliberate
blinks at 30.7, 29.2 and 126.7 frames per second, on one face within an
hour. It could not say why, because device, browser engine, camera,
orientation and mounting all moved together and nothing separated them.
`docs/validation-dry-run.txt` records that it could not.

HOW. `blinkStep` is a pure function of one aperture sample, one
timestamp and one threshold. So a blink can be built as an aperture
trace, sampled at a chosen rate, and run past the real detector with
nothing else changing. The PHASE is swept across a whole sample period,
200 offsets per point, because where the samples land relative to a
blink is the one thing nobody controls in a real session.

The blink model is a raised cosine down and another up, closing in a
third of the total and opening over the rest, which is the asymmetry the
dry run's amplitude-over-velocity figures show. The threshold and arm
line are the owner's own measured session `macbookair2`, which was
labelled P4 when this was written and is P5 now: baseline 7.78 mm, so the
blink line is 3.89 mm and the arm line, which is the blink line less the
10 percent hysteresis from fix #114, is 3.50 mm.

Reproduce with:

    BLINKLAB_PRINT_TABLE=1 npx vitest run test/core/blinkSampleRate.test.ts

open 7.0 mm, threshold 3.89 mm, arm line 3.50 mm

WHAT THIS ESTABLISHES
=====================

There are three regions, and only the middle one is about the rate.

1. DEEP BLINKS ARE FINE. At 2.80 mm and below, every rate from 25 Hz
   upward catches the blink at every phase. Nothing is broken for a
   firm blink.

2. TOO-SHALLOW BLINKS ARE NEVER CAUGHT, AT ANY RATE. At 3.60 mm the
   trace crosses the blink line, so the eye reads as closed, and never
   reaches the arm line, so it is never counted. This is a depth
   problem and more frames cannot fix it. It is also by design: fix
   #114 added the arm line precisely so that noise riding the blink
   line could not mint phantom blinks.

3. BETWEEN THEM IS A BAND, ABOUT 0.4 mm WIDE, WHERE THE RATE DECIDES.
   From 3.40 down to 3.00 mm, which is 16 to 35 ms spent below the arm
   line, the same blink is sometimes counted and sometimes not, and how
   often depends entirely on the sampling rate:

       min 3.30 mm     56% at 25 Hz    67% at 30 Hz    100% at 60 Hz

   Nothing about the person changed. Only where the samples happened to
   fall.

   Speed widens it. Held at one depth inside the band, an 80 ms blink is
   caught 43 percent of the time at 30 Hz and always at 90 Hz. A 250 ms
   blink at the same depth is always caught. Quick shallow blinks are
   the vulnerable ones.

THE MECHANISM IS THE ARM LINE, AND THAT IS PROVEN RATHER THAN ARGUED.
Setting APERTURE_HYSTERESIS_FRACTION to 0, which removes the arm line
and lets any crossing of the blink line count, turns three of the five
tests red and the band disappears. A second mutation, making the sampler
ignore its requested rate and always run at 1000 Hz, turns the two
rate-dependent tests red. So the tests are sensitive both to the cause
and to the variable.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT ESTABLISH
============================

IT DOES NOT PROVE THIS EXPLAINS THE DRY RUN'S 7 OF 10 AND 9 OF 10. It
shows the mechanism exists, and quantifies it. Whether real blinks live
in the 0.4 mm band often enough to account for those misses is a
different question, and the exports cannot answer it.

The reason is worth stating plainly, because it is a permanent limit on
this kind of evidence. THE DATA IS CENSORED. A blink the detector missed
writes no row at all, so every amplitude in every blink log is drawn
from the blinks that were caught. The population that would settle the
question is exactly the population the instrument cannot record. No
amount of re-reading the five sessions gets around it.

What the experiment does explain is the SHAPE of what was observed, and
that is not nothing:

- Misses arriving in runs rather than scattered, which both the phone
  and the laptop showed as clean multi-blink holes in a metronome
  cadence. Phase drifts slowly against a steady blink rhythm, so a
  marginal blinker sits on the wrong side of it for several blinks at a
  time.
- More misses on the slower machines and none on the fast one.
- The device that reported the SHORTEST blinks, the phone at a 96 ms
  median, missing the most of them, which is the direction the speed
  sweep predicts.

The model is synthetic. Real eyelid traces are noisier than a raised
cosine, and noise near the arm line would push detection in both
directions. The three regions and the direction of the effect do not
depend on the exact shape; the precise width of the band does.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE 25 FPS FLOOR
==================================

The floor is not the thing that is wrong. Refusing below 25 fps is
correct and the numbers above support it: at 25 Hz the band is at its
widest and a marginal blink is caught barely half the time.

What is wrong is that ABOVE the floor nothing is said at all. A session
at 29 fps and a session at 127 fps both pass the gate silently, and this
experiment shows they are not the same instrument. Between 25 and about
60 Hz, whether a marginal blink is counted is decided by the speed of
the viewer's computer.

That is the honest shape of remediation D1's remaining work, and it is
not the shape D1 was written in. D1 assumed a slow CAMERA declaring a
rate the page ignored. In five real sessions every camera declared 30
and the gate never wrongly opened once. The rate that varies is the
model's own, it is set by the machine, and the page reports it as a
readout rather than as a caveat on the numbers beside it.


THE SECOND RATE, 21 AUGUST 2026
===============================

Everything above sweeps ONE rate, the rate the page runs its detector
at, and reads a fresh aperture at every tick. That is an infinitely fast
camera. It is the right model for a clip, which is stepped frame by
frame, and it is the wrong model for a live camera.

A camera delivers frames on its own grid. Between two deliveries the
page can tick as often as it likes and the lid it reads does not move,
because it is re-reading the same photograph. So there are TWO rates,
and which one binds is a question this file did not ask.

WHY IT IS WORTH ASKING. The camera path in `src/io/frameLoop.ts` is
`requestAnimationFrame` only, with no check that a new frame arrived,
and `src/io/camera.ts` requests a resolution and no frame rate at all.
The same file already refuses to make this mistake for CLIPS, and its
comment says why: ticking at display rate and reading an interpolated
clock "would report the display's refresh rate as the clip's frame
rate", which is why a clip is driven by the frames themselves. The
camera never got that treatment. Every one of the twelve measured
sessions declared a 30 fps camera while the page reported processing
rates from 24.4 to 126.7.

THE MODEL. Sample and hold. The delivery grid runs at deliveryHz; the
processing grid runs at processHz; at each processing tick the detector
reads the aperture of the most recent DELIVERED frame. Both phases are
swept, because neither is controlled in a real session: where the
camera's grid falls against the blink, and where the processing grid
falls against the camera's. The old model is kept as the special case
deliveryHz = Infinity, and a test pins the two against each other so
the new one cannot quietly move the published numbers above.

Reproduce with the same command as the table above, which prints these
two tables beneath it.

THE SWEEP BUDGET, because the digits below depend on it. Both phases
are swept on a grid of 200 camera offsets by 16 processing offsets.
That is measured rather than picked: at the first budget tried, 50 by
8, roughly half of these cells were wrong in the second decimal, so
the sweep resolved about a fiftieth while the table printed a
hundredth. 200 by 16 and 200 by 32 agree exactly, and 400 by 32 moves
no cell by more than 0.0025. Read the second decimal as carrying about
that much residual. Raising the PROCESSING offsets past 16 changes
nothing at all, which is itself the finding: the phase that decides
what was photographed is the camera's, not the reader's.

WHAT THE MODEL SAYS
-------------------

1. ABOVE THE DELIVERED RATE, PROCESSING BUYS NOTHING. 30.7, 55 and
   126.7 frames per second are the same column. The 0.4 mm band does
   not close; it stops closing at the camera.

2. THE IMPROVEMENT LIVES IN THE CAMERA. The same blink at the same
   processing rate goes from 0.48 to 0.96 when the camera delivers 60
   instead of 30. That is the whole of what a "faster machine" was
   credited with.

3. THE REASSURING REGION IS CONDITIONAL, and this is new. The table
   above says deep blinks at 2.80 mm are caught at every rate from 25
   up. With delivery modelled, a machine processing at 25 on a 30 fps
   camera catches that same firm blink 0.87 of the time: below the
   delivered rate the processing grid skips delivered frames, and a
   held value can fall entirely between two ticks.

   AND DEPTH BARELY RESCUES IT, which is why the table carries two
   rows far below anything the band needed. At 25 Hz processing on a
   30 fps camera the same 150 ms blink reads 0.87 at 2.80 mm, 0.91 at
   2.40 and 0.94 at 2.00: driving the lid almost shut buys seven
   points. What does rescue it is DURATION. The harness's own deep
   control, 1.5 mm over 180 ms rather than 150, returns 1.00 at every
   processing rate on the same 30 fps camera. So the quantity that
   fails below the delivered rate is how long the lid stayed under the
   arm line, not how far under it went.

   Region 1 of the 17 August table is therefore true for a camera
   faster than the processing rate, and this is the first thing that
   would make the 25 fps floor itself look too low. NOTHING IS CHANGED
   ON THAT BASIS HERE: a floor moved because a synthetic table
   suggested it would be a constant chosen from a model rather than
   from a measurement.

THE PREDICTION, COMMITTED BEFORE ANY DEVICE IS OPENED
-----------------------------------------------------

This model is not yet measured against anything. The dry run recorded
processing rates and never recorded a delivered rate, because nothing
in the instrument measures one. So the following is written down first,
in the arrangement this project uses for every question it cares about.

What is predicted, for the four sessions whose baseline is sound AND
whose marks are where the protocol asks, which is the set the dry
run's own §2 table uses. The dry run has FIVE sound sessions; P1
`iphone` is set aside here for its marks, not for its ruler, and that
is said out loud because a prediction section that quietly skips a
session which MISSED blinks would invite exactly the reading that it
was skipped for being inconvenient. P1 is a case this model should
eventually account for too.

    macbookair2      29.2 processing    delivers about 30, read
                                        fraction below 1: the machine
                                        is slower than the camera and
                                        skips delivered frames
    iphone2          30.7 processing    delivers about 30
    iphone17promax   55.0 processing    delivers about 60
    pcsony          126.7 processing    delivers 60 or more

THE DECISIVE CASE IS pcsony, AND IT CAN REFUTE THE MODEL OUTRIGHT.
It caught 10 of 10 while processing at 126.7. If its camera delivered
only 30, then under this model it had exactly the evidence a 30 fps
machine has, and it should have missed blinks like `iphone2` did. It
did not. So either that chain delivers 60 or more — a Sony A7 IV
through a Cam Link 4K can — or the delivery model does not explain the
dry run, and section 1 above is wrong about which rate binds.

If pcsony delivers 30 and still caught 10 of 10, THIS SECTION IS
RETRACTED in a new dated section rather than quietly amended, and the
existing claims below stand as they are.

WHAT MOVES UNDER EACH OUTCOME. The claim that the processing rate is
the rate that matters is recorded in seven places, and a correction has
to reach all of them or the project will have corrected the page and
left the record wrong, which is the defect this file's own project
history is full of:

    src/core/fpsGate.ts        "The camera is not the cause and a
                               faster machine would count more"
    README.md                  the "how fast your computer is" bullet
    MODEL_CARD.md              "the machine rather than the camera",
                               and the tested-on paragraph
    STATE.md                   "The processing rate is set by the
                               COMPUTER, not the camera"
    validation-dry-run.txt §3  the same sentence, as a finding
    validation-dry-run.txt §4  "The phone was never the problem. The
                               rate was."
    REMEDIATION.md D1          closed on the reasoning that every
                               camera declared 30 and the gate never
                               wrongly opened

    IF DELIVERY BINDS: every one of those gets a dated correction
    naming this file, and §4 keeps its conclusion while losing its
    mechanism — the rate WAS the cause, and the iPhone 17 very likely
    ran a 60 fps camera, so the prediction that was committed there
    still held for a reason it did not name. A prediction that holds
    for the wrong mechanism is still a prediction that held, and it is
    recorded as such rather than reread as a mistake.

    IF DELIVERY DOES NOT BIND: nothing above moves, and this section
    is the thing that was wrong.

WHAT THE 25 FPS GATE DOES, DECIDED NOW RATHER THAN AFTER THE NUMBER IS
SEEN. The gate reads the processing rate today. If any device is
measured delivering below 25 while processing above it, the gate is
refusing on the wrong quantity and moves to the delivered rate, and
D1's closure gets a dated correction saying its premise did occur after
all. If no device does — which is what is expected, since every
declared rate was 30 — the gate stays exactly where it is, because a
gate moved on a model rather than on an observation is the same error
in the other direction.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT ESTABLISH
----------------------------

THE HARNESS BYPASSES THE FACE MODEL, and that is an assumption, not a
fact. It assumes re-processing an identical delivered frame at a later
timestamp yields the same aperture. MediaPipe runs in VIDEO mode with
temporal tracking, so a repeated frame at an advancing timestamp may
give slightly different landmarks. The reasoning for the model is that
a repeated photograph carries no NEW information about where the lid
was, so tracking can smooth but cannot add evidence; the reasoning
against it is that smoothing is not nothing.

THE FALSIFIER, NAMED AND NOT YET RUN: measure one clip twice through
the real page, once stepped and once watched. Stepped seeks to every
frame; watched plays in real time and re-reads frames on a fast
machine. Both modes already exist and both are already driven by
`test/e2e/videoFile.spec.ts`. If a repeated frame carried new
information, the watched export would show apertures the stepped one
does not. It needs no camera, no dataset and no people, and it belongs
to whoever runs this on a machine with the full browser toolchain.

Until that runs, section 1's claim is a claim about the SAMPLER, proven
against the real `blinkStep`, and not a claim about the whole pipeline.

MUTATIONS, so the new tests are known to be sensitive to the mechanism
rather than only to themselves. Making the delivery stage ignore its
requested rate turns two of the four new tests red: "processing faster
than the camera buys nothing" and "a faster CAMERA does what a faster
machine was credited with". Making the delivery grid a thousand times
finer turns the same two red. The other two survive both mutations,
correctly: one is a direction check that holds under either model, and
the other is the pin that holds the infinite-camera case to the 17
August numbers.
